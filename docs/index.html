An industry report meets the filings

Does oil really drive Varun Beverages' margins?

By Aeternifrigus ↗

A report on Indian packaged water said yes, dramatically. I tested its claims against 38 quarters of company filings, oil prices and weather with a reproducible machine learning pipeline. Oil matters, but far less than claimed.

How this started

From a 20 rupee bottle of water to a model that is allowed to say no.

  1. The question

    A bottle that sells for ₹20 and costs about ₹3 to make

    It began as a simple question: how does a bottle of water that sells for ₹20, and that one report put at ₹3 to ₹4 at the factory gate, turn into a business? I wrote three reports to find out. They covered the unit economics (a plastic preform at roughly ₹1.50 to ₹1.80), the retail cascade (the shopkeeper keeps 40 to 50 percent), the 100 km limit that keeps water local because it is heavy and cheap, and the 20 litre jar that quietly subsidises the bottle.

  2. The reports

    Reports that sounded sure

    The reports were confident. They used words like "proof" and "near perfect correlation": a 42 percent summer surge, r = 0.89 between heat and sales, and a gross margin that drops to 38 percent once oil passes $90. Confident writing is not evidence, though. The statistical evidence was a four-row table and a regression chart that existed only as a caption.

    So the reports became hypotheses. Every claim went into a table next to the number it implied, ready to be checked.

  3. The data

    Finding data that could say no

    Packaged water is not reported on its own. The closest public numbers belong to Varun Beverages, PepsiCo's bottler in India, and it sells mostly soft drinks: water was 16 to 22 percent of its volume in the quarters I checked. That turned the project from "the packaged water industry" into "Varun Beverages", and it is why this page is careful about what it claims. Quarterly results came from company filings, oil and exchange rates from FRED, weather from Open-Meteo.

  4. The mistake

    The first answer looked fine and was wrong

    A first pass pulled 30 quarters out of the results PDFs and trained a model. The outputs were strange: a negative fixed cost, and a model that lost to a simple baseline. They were symptoms, not findings. Checking six quarters against the published figures showed EBITDA margins of about 5 percent where the company reported 22 to 28. The extractor had read the wrong lines of the statement.

  5. The fix

    Making the data hard to get wrong

    The table was rebuilt from the original PDFs. Their text layer turned out to be damaged (decimal points came out as commas), so every number is read by its shape, and every column has to add up to the filing's own totals or it is thrown out. Five of the PDFs were scans that nothing could read, so two quarters were entered by hand from page images and checked the same way.

    The test that mattered: the finished table reproduces the gross margins the company itself reported for six second quarters, to within 0.04 points.

  6. The model

    A fair fight

    With trustworthy data the model could finally be tested. The first version tied a simple baseline, and the reason was that it knew nothing about seasons: gross margin has a strong yearly pattern, and the baseline got it for free. Once the model had quarter-of-year terms I switched feature groups on and off. Oil cut the error by 12 percent beyond seasonality. Weather made it worse.

    The oil effect is 0.43 points of gross margin per $10 of Brent, against at least 7 implied by the report.

  7. The system

    From analysis to something that is operated

    A result nobody can reproduce is an anecdote. So the project got hash-pinned installs, tests that run the whole pipeline on synthetic data with a planted signal, a monthly job that refreshes the data and runs drift checks, a nowcast for the current quarter, and releases that refuse a stale model. The nowcast now carries a warning: Brent has moved above anything the model was trained on, so it says so instead of giving a confident number.

  8. Still open

    What this does not settle

    Whether heat really drives volume is untested, because the filings do not include organic volume. The sample is 38 quarters from one company. And the oil effect is evidence about size, not proof of cause. The next step is to get volume by quarter from investor presentations and test heat properly.

Three habits worth keeping

  • Treat a confident report as a hypothesis. Write each claim down with the number it implies, then try to break it.
  • Check your data against numbers the company published before trusting any model built on it. The model's first strange outputs were data errors.
  • Let the model be unimpressive when that is the truth. The finding is "much smaller than claimed", not "confirmed".

The claim

Three reports about the Indian packaged water industry made specific, testable statements. Each one became a hypothesis to check against real data. Varun Beverages (VBL), the PepsiCo bottler, is the best public data source for it, though it sells mostly soft drinks.

Hypothesis 1

Heat drives sales

Summer heatwaves lift Q2 volume by about 42% and correlate with it at r = 0.89.

Hypothesis 2

Oil crushes margins

When Brent is above $90, gross margin falls toward 38%. Below $70 it sits above 52%.

Hypothesis 3

Margins swing with input costs

EBITDA margin is 21.8% in Q2 but only 13.5% in Q3 and 14.2% in Q1, because of costs.

Verdicts come from claims/claims.csv. Hypothesis 1 can only be partly checked: the filings do not include organic volume, so it stays open.

Test 1: does margin collapse when oil is expensive?

Each dot is one quarter of VBL's filings: the average price of Brent crude against that quarter's gross margin. If the report were right, the dots on the right would fall toward 38%.

How the machine learning tests it

A scatter plot cannot separate oil from the seasons. Gross margin is higher in some quarters for reasons that have nothing to do with oil. So I let a model try to predict margin before it happens, and measured whether knowing the oil price helps.

  1. Turn filings into clean numbers.VBL's results PDFs are parsed into 38 quarters. Every column must add up to the filing's own totals, or it is rejected, and the margins match what the company itself reported.
  2. Give the model only what is known in advance.Brent in rupees over the last four quarters, plus which quarter of the year it is. Nothing from the future.
  3. Keep the model simple on purpose.A regularised linear model (ridge). With 38 quarters a complex model would just memorise noise, and a linear one shows directly how many margin points move per unit of oil.
  4. Backtest as if it were the past again.For each of the last 26 quarters, train only on earlier quarters, predict that quarter, then compare with what really happened and with a naive guess (the same quarter last year).
  5. Switch features off one group at a time.If oil truly matters, removing it should make predictions worse. That ablation is the actual test of the hypothesis.
  6. Put an honest error bar on the answer.A block bootstrap resamples runs of consecutive quarters to see how much the estimate would wobble.
  7. Keep watching.A monthly job refreshes the data, flags when oil leaves the range the model has seen, and warns when errors grow.

Prediction against reality

What this does and does not show

It shows that the report's magnitude is not supported by 38 quarters of filings, and that oil has a small, consistent negative link to gross margin that holds up out of sample.

It does not show cause and effect. It is one company, mostly soft drinks. Weather and volume are untested here. The feature set was chosen while looking at this same backtest, so the improvement is slightly optimistic. And Brent is now above the range the model was trained on (see Live status). Treat this as strong evidence against the report's size, not a proof of the true size.

Test 2: why do margins swing through the year?

The report credited input costs. The filings point somewhere else: most costs are fixed, so when summer volume is high they are spread over more sales.
